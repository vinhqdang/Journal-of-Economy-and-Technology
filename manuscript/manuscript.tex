\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Whether richer or poorer economies gain more from information and communication technology (ICT) and, more recently, artificial intelligence (AI) is usually answered by comparing estimates across studies, which differ in samples, measures and estimators. \textbf{Methods.} We collected the comparisons that studies make themselves: estimates of the same technology--outcome association, in the same specification, for a richer and a poorer group of economies. Studies came from a pilot evidence map of 97 cross-country studies (OpenAlex, Crossref, NBER, Google Scholar) and from supplementary searches aimed at income comparisons (backward and forward citation searching and a targeted OpenAlex query). Contrasts were coded with language-model assistance, and every headline contrast used in the meta-analysis was verified by the author against the source table. The effect size is the difference in partial correlations between the richer and the poorer group, pooled by random effects with one contrast per study. \textbf{Results.} Of 164 eligible studies (97 from the main search and 67 from the searches aimed at income comparisons), 82 report at least one richer-versus-poorer contrast. In their headline contrast, 35 find a larger benefit in the richer group, 27 in the poorer group and 12 find opposite signs (sign test $p = 0.37$). For the 44 contrasts with enough information, the pooled difference is close to zero; excluding eight contrasts flagged as implausible because their source tables are internally inconsistent, it is $-0.001$ (95\% CI $-0.049$ to 0.047). For output and productivity the difference is $-0.038$ ($-0.074$ to $-0.001$; 24 studies), slightly favouring poorer economies, but the interval reaches zero when flagged contrasts are excluded. The average association itself, in 22 studies, is a partial correlation of 0.16. \textbf{Conclusions.} Studies that compare income groups within the same design do not show that richer economies gain more from digital technologies; any difference is small relative to the average association. Most studies are at serious risk of bias, and few test the difference formally.
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} digital technology; artificial intelligence; income level; meta-analysis; productivity.\\
\textbf{JEL codes:} O33, O47, O11, L96.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Whether new technologies narrow or widen income gaps between countries is a long-standing question, and it has returned with artificial intelligence. Early empirical work found sizeable output effects of telecommunications infrastructure \cite{roller2001}. Work on the long-run diffusion of technologies finds that technologies now arrive at similar times in rich and poor countries, but that the intensity of their use diverges \cite{comin2010,comin2018}. If so, the gain from a technology should depend on a country's conditions, and the question of who gains more is an empirical one.

Two existing meta-analyses address it by comparing estimates across studies. A meta-regression of 59 econometric studies finds that developed countries gained more from computers than developing ones \cite{stanley2018}, and a meta-analysis of infrastructure studies finds larger digital effects in developed countries \cite{foster2026}. Comparisons across studies, however, mix the effect of income with everything else that differs between studies: the sample period, the technology measure, the outcome and the estimator. Studies that cover poorer economies can differ systematically from those that cover richer ones in all of these respects, so a difference across studies can therefore reflect how the studies were done rather than who gains more.

Many primary studies answer the question more directly. They estimate the same specification separately for richer and poorer groups of economies, or add an interaction with income. Such within-study comparisons hold the design constant, in the same way as within-trial subgroup comparisons are preferred to across-trial comparisons in medical meta-analysis \cite{fisher2017}. They have not been collected. This paper collects them and asks: \emph{when studies compare richer and poorer economies within the same design, is the association between digital technology and economic outcomes larger in richer economies?}

We combine a pilot evidence map of 97 cross-country studies with supplementary searches aimed at income comparisons, and code 363 richer-versus-poorer contrasts from 82 studies. Our answer is no. Studies are split almost evenly on which group gains more, and the pooled difference in partial correlations is close to zero, with an interval that excludes differences of more than about a third of the average association. For output and productivity, the point estimate slightly favours poorer economies. The evidence is weak in other respects: most studies are at serious risk of bias, few test the difference formally, and the poorest economies are often pooled with lower-middle income ones. But it does not support the view that the gains from digital technology accrue mainly to richer economies.

The paper makes two contributions. It is, to our knowledge, the first synthesis of within-study income comparisons for digital technologies, which gives a cleaner answer to the distributional question than across-study comparisons. And it maps the wider cross-country evidence on digital technologies by outcome, technology, income coverage and risk of bias, which shows where better evidence is needed.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the methods, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them. Sections~\ref{sec:limits} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} give the limitations and conclusions.

\section{Why the same technology could pay differently}\label{sec:background}
Four mechanisms recur in the literature on information and communication technology and on automation, and each implies a reason why the gain from a technology might differ by income level.

\emph{Infrastructure and complementary investment.} Telecommunications infrastructure raises output, and the effect may depend on how much infrastructure already exists, because network benefits grow with the size of the network \cite{roller2001}. Where electricity, roads and financial services are scarce, the same handset or connection may yield less. This implies that the gain depends on a country's stock of complementary assets, which is usually lower in poorer economies.

\emph{Information and market access.} Mobile phones can reduce price dispersion and waste where markets are thin. In a study of South Indian fisheries, the arrival of mobile phones reduced price dispersion and waste and raised welfare \cite{jensen2007}, and a review of evidence from Africa describes how mobile phones can improve information flows in agricultural and other markets \cite{aker2010}. These mechanisms are strongest where information was previously scarce, which is more common in lower-income settings, and they imply large early gains at low adoption levels.

\emph{Skills and tasks.} Computers substitute for routine tasks and complement non-routine tasks \cite{autor2003}, and automation both displaces and creates tasks \cite{acemoglu2019}. In Africa, the arrival of fast internet through submarine cables was associated with higher employment, and the effect was larger for more skilled workers \cite{hjort2019}. The gains therefore depend on the skills of the workforce, which differ by income level.

\emph{Absorptive capacity.} The ability to use external knowledge depends on prior related knowledge \cite{cohen1990}. For technology adoption this means that returns depend on human capital, management capacity and institutions, again variables that differ systematically by income.

Two forces therefore pull in different directions. Catch-up and early information gains suggest that low-adoption, lower-income economies could gain more per unit of adoption. Complementary assets, skills and absorptive capacity suggest that higher-income economies could gain more. A third consideration is time: the long-run diffusion evidence suggests that gains may build over years, and a short window may miss them \cite{comin2018}. A review of the empirical evidence on information technology and economic performance, published early in the period we study, describes the findings as mixed \cite{dedrick2003}; it is not an estimating study and is not among our included studies.


\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Design and protocol}
The study began as a systematic review following PRISMA 2020 \cite{prisma2020} and the structure of PRISMA-P \cite{prismap}, with a written protocol (version 1.0, 2 October 2026) available through the data and code link in the Declarations. The first stage produced a pilot evidence map of cross-country studies. Because those studies could not answer the income question by comparison across studies, the analysis was refocused on within-study income contrasts, and supplementary searches aimed at studies that make such comparisons were added. The protocol was not registered, and the changes are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:amend}. PRISMA 2020 items are used as a reporting guide (Appendix~\ref{app:prisma}).

\subsection{Eligibility}
Studies were eligible if they estimate, with their own data, the association between adoption, diffusion, investment or capability of ICT, mobile telephony, internet or broadband, crypto-assets or AI (including robots) and at least one outcome in six families: (1) output and productivity, (2) structural change, (3) labour market, (4) poverty and distribution, (5) living standards beyond income (health, schooling, financial inclusion, human development) and (6) resource cost (electricity use, carbon emissions or intensity). Studies of earlier technologies needed at least 10 economies or an explicit income-group comparison, and AI studies at least 3 economies. Reviews, theory, single-country studies and studies whose only outcome is adoption were excluded. Full criteria are in Appendix~\ref{app:criteria}.

For the meta-analysis, a study contributes if it reports a \emph{richer-versus-poorer contrast}: the same specification estimated for a richer and a poorer group of economies (World Bank income groups, developed and developing, OECD and non-OECD, or the authors' own income split), or an interaction of the technology with an income group or income level. Groups that are not ordered by income (regions, ICT-intensity groups) do not count.

\subsection{Searches}
The main search covered OpenAlex (a Boolean query restricted to the economics field), Crossref (96 technology-by-context queries) and NBER working papers from 1995 to 2 October 2026, supplemented by the first page of eight Google Scholar queries (Appendix~\ref{app:search}). Scopus, Web of Science and EconLit were not available.

Two supplementary searches targeted income comparisons. First, backward and forward citation searching \cite{wohlin2014} in OpenAlex from the 32 studies in the evidence map that compare income groups and from the earlier meta-analysis of ICT and growth \cite{stanley2018}. Second, an OpenAlex query without a field restriction that combines technology and outcome terms with income-group terms such as ``income groups'', ``low-income countries'' and ``developed and developing'' (Appendix~\ref{app:search}). Records already screened in the main search were removed.

\subsection{Screening and full texts}
In both searches, automated rules first removed records with no eligible technology term, no outcome term or no multi-economy cue; titles and abstracts were then screened against the criteria with language-model assistance, and every screening decision was verified by the author. In the main search, a blind re-screening of a random sample of 340 records by a separate model run gave 79\% agreement at the abstract stage (kappa 0.59). In the supplementary searches only records that were includable and reported an income comparison were taken to full text. Full texts were obtained from open-access sources or supplied by the author; records without a full text were not used.

\subsection{Coding of contrasts}
For each study we recorded every richer-versus-poorer contrast: the outcome, technology, estimator, groups, coefficients with standard errors or $t$ statistics and the number of observations in each group (or for the interaction), whether the study tests the difference, and the direction of the difference in terms of benefit (for inequality, poverty, emissions and other outcomes where lower is better, a larger reduction counts as a larger benefit). One contrast per study was designated as headline by a fixed rule: the output and productivity family first, otherwise the study's main family; the main technology measure; the preferred specification; and the richest against the poorest groups available. Coding was done by a language model reading the full text, with a table locator and a quote for each value. Every headline contrast used in the meta-analysis was then checked against the source table by a second model run and verified by the author. Contrasts whose source tables are internally inconsistent (for example, $t$ values above 40 without significance stars) were flagged as implausible.

\subsection{Effect size and synthesis}
Each group estimate was converted to a partial correlation, $r = t/\sqrt{t^2 + df}$, with $df$ equal to the group's observations minus 10 \cite{stanley2012}. The contrast is $d = r_{\mathrm{richer}} - r_{\mathrm{poorer}}$, oriented so that a positive value means a larger benefit in the richer group, with variance equal to the sum of the two group variances, $(1-r^2)^2/df$, since the groups are disjoint samples. For interaction designs, $d$ is the partial correlation of the interaction term, signed by direction. Headline contrasts were pooled by random effects \cite{dersimonian1986} with Hartung--Knapp--Sidik--Jonkman intervals, which perform better with few and heterogeneous studies \cite{hartung2001,inthout2014}. Because many studies report only coefficients and significance stars, we also report a sign test on the direction of all headline contrasts. Sensitivity analyses restrict the sample to contrasts not flagged, contrasts checked against the full text, split-sample designs, World Bank income groups, formally tested differences, studies not rated critical and each search, and impute missing observation counts.

\subsection{Risk of bias}
Risk of bias used a six-domain tool adapted from ROBINS-I \cite{robinsi}: confounding and reverse causation, exposure measurement, selection of countries and years, outcome measurement, specification and researcher degrees of freedom, and selective reporting, with an overall rating of low, moderate, serious or critical (Appendix~\ref{app:criteria}). Ratings for the studies in the evidence map were made by one rater; studies added by the supplementary searches were rated on the same scale by a model run calibrated on rated examples from the evidence map.

\subsection{The wider evidence}
For the 97 studies in the evidence map we also describe the evidence by outcome family, technology, income coverage and design \cite{swim}, and compute one descriptive pooled association between ICT-type adoption and output from the headline estimate of 22 studies, each checked by hand against the source paper.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Studies}
The main search yielded 3,612 records after deduplication; 143 full texts were read and 97 studies were included in the evidence map (Figure~\ref{fig:prisma}). The supplementary searches yielded 3,595 new records from citation searching and 2,280 from the targeted query. After the automated rules, 1,759 were screened, 563 were includable or possibly includable and 181 of these reported an income comparison. Full texts of 82 were read, 67 were eligible and 15 were excluded (10 for an ineligible exposure such as a general financial-inclusion index, 3 for an ineligible outcome, 2 for a single country or too few economies). The other 99 could not be obtained. In total, 164 studies are eligible: 97 from the main search and 67 from the supplementary searches.

Of the 164 eligible studies, 82 report at least one richer-versus-poorer contrast: 27 from the evidence map and 55 from the supplementary searches. They provide 363 contrasts. In the headline contrast, 73 studies estimate separate samples and 9 use an interaction; 52 compare World Bank income groups, 24 developed and developing (or advanced and emerging) economies, 4 OECD and non-OECD economies and 2 use the authors' income threshold. In 37 headline contrasts the poorer group contains low-income economies; in the others it is lower-middle or middle income, or developing economies as a whole. Only 15 studies test the difference between groups formally. Forty-four headline contrasts report enough to compute an effect size. The author verified all 44 against the source tables, and none needed correction; eight were flagged as implausible because the source tables are internally inconsistent. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:capp} lists all headline contrasts.

\subsection{Do richer economies gain more?}
Table~\ref{tab:cdirs} shows the direction of the headline contrasts. In 35 studies the benefit is larger in the richer group, in 27 in the poorer group, and in 12 the two groups have opposite signs; 8 find no clear difference or cannot be classified. The split between richer and poorer is not distinguishable from an even one (sign test, $p = 0.37$). The pattern is similar across families: for output and productivity, the family with most studies, 19 favour the richer and 17 the poorer group.

Table~\ref{tab:csens} and Figure~\ref{fig:cforest} give the effect sizes. Pooled over all 44 contrasts, the difference in partial correlations is $+0.035$ (95\% CI $-0.077$ to 0.148) and very heterogeneous, because a few contrasts with implausibly small standard errors dominate the variance. Excluding the eight flagged contrasts, the difference is $-0.001$ (95\% CI $-0.049$ to 0.047). Since the average association between ICT-type adoption and output in the wider evidence is a partial correlation of about 0.16 (Section~\ref{sec:wider}), these intervals rule out income differences larger than about a third of the average association.

For output and productivity the difference is $-0.038$ ($-0.074$ to $-0.001$; 24 studies), so the association is slightly larger in poorer economies; excluding flagged contrasts gives $-0.037$ ($-0.077$ to 0.003). The result is consistent across the samples in Table~\ref{tab:csens}: no restriction produces a positive and significant difference. The few studies that test the difference formally give $-0.007$. Studies from the main search give $-0.016$ and those from the supplementary searches $+0.052$, both with intervals that include zero; the latter are much more heterogeneous.

\subsection{Risk of bias}
No study was rated at low risk of bias. Of the 164 eligible studies, 17 were rated moderate (10\%), 131 serious (80\%) and 16 critical (10\%); the distribution is similar in the evidence map (11, 75 and 11) and in the studies added by the supplementary searches (6, 56 and 5). The commonest weaknesses are adoption treated as exogenous, with internal-instrument GMM as the only strategy, composite indices whose scope does not match the technology, and many specifications with no stated preferred one. Restricting the meta-analysis to studies not rated critical does not change the result (Table~\ref{tab:csens}).

\subsection{The wider evidence}\label{sec:wider}
This section describes the 97 studies from the main search only. The 67 added studies were sought because they compare income groups, so adding them would overstate the share of studies with income comparisons, low-income coverage or split samples in the literature. The 97 studies were published between 2003 and 2027, 78 of them from 2020 onward (Figure~\ref{fig:years}). Eighty study ICT, mobile, internet or broadband, 15 AI and 2 both. Table~\ref{tab:design} compares the two streams and Table~\ref{tab:families} the outcome families. Most studies report positive associations with output and productivity (47 of 53), but mixed, null and negative results are common in every family.

\emph{The average association.} For the 22 studies with a convertible headline estimate of ICT-type adoption on output, the random-effects pooled partial correlation is 0.161 (95\% CI 0.093 to 0.228), with $I^2 = 93\%$ and a prediction interval from $-0.144$ to 0.465. This is of the same order as the earlier meta-regression of ICT and growth \cite{stanley2018}, but it falls to between 0.04 and 0.09 after adjustment for small-study bias \cite{stanley2014}, and it moves between 0.138 and 0.173 when single studies are dropped (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}). Moderator regressions changed sign as studies were added and are not interpreted.

\emph{Conditions.} Of 181 heterogeneity findings in the evidence map, 145 are moderation tests (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Human capital and infrastructure are most often found to amplify the association, and governance and trade openness give mixed results. The studies state 29 thresholds in 21 studies, in different units and each resting on one study.

\input{tab_conditions.tex}

\emph{Gaps.} Low-income economies are listed in 35 of the 97 studies, and in only 2 of the 17 AI studies (Fisher exact test against the other studies, $p = 0.03$). AI studies use readiness indices, patents, robot density or occupational exposure rather than realised use, start in 2012 or later, and are more often rated critical (24\% against 9\%). None measures the use of generative AI at country level.

\subsection{A check with public data}\label{sec:panel}
To see how a simple version of the income question behaves under different specifications, we estimated the association between mobile adoption and growth of GDP per capita by income group with World Bank data for 1995 to 2019 (Table~\ref{tab:panelshort}). In five-year fixed-effects panels, the same-period association is positive in all income groups and largest in lower-middle income economies, but the predetermined specification, which uses the previous period's change, is positive only in lower-middle income economies and negative in high-income ones. A staggered event study with not-yet-treated controls \cite{callaway2021} and local projections \cite{jorda2005} give further different pictures, and no income group is positive under all four designs. Like the studies, the check does not support a larger gain in richer economies, and it shows that a single specification cannot settle the question.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Who gains more?}
When studies compare richer and poorer economies within the same design, they do not find that richer economies gain more from digital technologies. The directions are split almost evenly, the pooled difference is close to zero, and the interval excludes differences larger than about a third of the average association. For output and productivity, the evidence leans slightly towards poorer economies, which is consistent with catch-up and with large early gains where information was scarce \cite{jensen2007,aker2010}, but the difference is small and fragile.

This differs from the conclusion of across-study comparisons, in which developed countries appear to gain more \cite{stanley2018,foster2026}. The two are not necessarily in conflict. Across studies, income is confounded with period, technology measure and estimator; within studies these are held constant. The contrast between the two approaches suggests that part of the apparent advantage of richer economies in earlier syntheses reflects differences between the studies rather than between the economies, as with across-trial comparisons of subgroups in medicine \cite{fisher2017}. The within-study evidence has its own weakness: most designs do not identify causal effects, and a common bias in both groups would cancel only if it is of equal size.

\subsection{What this means}
For policy, the evidence does not support the view that digital technologies mainly benefit economies that are already rich, nor does it show that they close income gaps. Conditions matter more than income group as such: human capital and infrastructure are the moderators most often found to raise the association, which is consistent with the view that use, not arrival, drives the gains \cite{comin2018}. For AI, the evidence is too thin and too indirect to say who will gain more.

For research, three improvements would help. Studies that compare income groups should test the difference and report standard errors and observation counts for each group; fewer than one in five does so now. The poorest economies should be analysed as a group of their own rather than pooled with lower-middle income ones. And designs that treat adoption as exogenous should give way to strategies that use variation in the timing or cost of access, such as the arrival of submarine cables \cite{hjort2019}.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Search.} Scopus, Web of Science and EconLit were not available. The supplementary searches relied on OpenAlex and citation links, and full texts could not be obtained for 99 of the 181 records with income comparisons, mostly paywalled; published studies with significant contrasts may be over-represented.
\item \textbf{Screening.} Titles and abstracts were screened with language-model assistance; every decision was verified by the author, but there was no second independent human screener. In the supplementary searches, only records that reported an income comparison were read in full, so the 67 added studies are not a sample of the wider literature.
\item \textbf{Coding.} Contrasts were coded with language-model assistance. The author verified all 44 headline contrasts used in the meta-analysis against the source tables, with no corrections, but no contrast was coded independently by a second person, and the direction of contrasts without an effect size rests on one coding.
\item \textbf{Effect size.} Partial correlations depend on an assumed number of regressors and on group sample sizes, and many studies report only coefficients and stars; half of the headline contrasts could not be converted. Group definitions differ across studies, and the poorer group often includes lower-middle income economies.
\item \textbf{Design quality.} No study has low risk of bias. Within-study contrasts remove differences in design between groups but not a bias that differs between groups. Risk-of-bias ratings for the added studies were made by a calibrated model run.
\item \textbf{Registration.} The protocol was not registered, and the analysis of income contrasts was added after the first stage.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Comparisons made within studies, with the design held constant, do not show that richer economies gain more from digital technologies than poorer ones. Of 82 studies, the split between those finding a larger gain in richer and in poorer economies is close to even, and the pooled difference in partial correlations is close to zero. For output and productivity, the association is, if anything, slightly larger in poorer economies. The evidence base is large but weak: most studies are at serious risk of bias, few test the difference, and evidence on AI in low-income economies is almost absent. Future studies should test income differences formally, analyse the poorest economies separately and use designs that identify the effect of access.

\section{Sensitivity of the pooled figure and the moderator regressions}\label{app:sens}
This appendix reports the analyses behind the descriptive pooled association in Section~\ref{sec:results}, including the moderator regressions that we do not interpret. All use the 148 convertible estimates from 24 studies (22 studies with a headline estimate), with standard errors clustered by study; with so few clusters the clustered standard errors are optimistic.

\emph{Stability to the studies included.} A first run with 103 estimates from 18 studies gave a pooled partial correlation of 0.189 (95\% CI 0.104 to 0.274) from 16 studies with a headline estimate; the present run gives 0.161 (0.093 to 0.228). Dropping each study in turn moves the pooled value between 0.138 and 0.173. Excluding studies rated critical gives 0.146 (20 studies, 95\% CI 0.077 to 0.215); excluding one of two studies by the same authors on the same panel gives 0.160. Restricting to the 13 studies with an identification strategy beyond controls gives 0.184 (0.107 to 0.261, $I^2=89\%$). The pooled value is stable to the degrees-of-freedom assumption (0.158 to 0.163 for 0, 5 and 20 regressors).

\emph{Moderator regressions.} Table~\ref{tab:mra} shows the regression of partial correlations on an indicator of a low- or middle-income sample, an indicator of system or difference GMM, and technology type. In the first run the GMM coefficient was $-0.16$ ($p<0.01$) and the internet coefficient $+0.14$ ($p=0.09$); after studies found later were added they are $+0.07$ ($p=0.37$) and $-0.08$ ($p=0.15$). Coefficients whose sign changes when a few studies are added are not findings, and we draw no conclusion about estimators, technologies or income settings from them. Low- and middle-income samples contribute 10 studies (33 estimates), against 21 studies and 115 estimates for high-income or mixed samples (mean partial correlation 0.146 against 0.114, unweighted; in the first run, 0.031 against 0.123).

\emph{Small-study bias.} The funnel-asymmetry test slope on the standard error is 1.60 ($p=0.11$) with a bias-corrected intercept of 0.036 ($p=0.47$); the precision-effect estimate with standard error gives 0.086 ($p=0.03$). In the first run the slope was 1.91 ($p=0.06$) and the corrected values 0.005 and 0.064. Evidence of selective reporting is therefore weak, but the corrected values (0.04 to 0.09) lie well below the uncorrected 0.16 (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}).

\section{Protocol amendments}\label{app:amend}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item (2 Oct 2026) The OpenAlex query was written with explicit phrase variants and restricted to the economics field, because an unrestricted query returned about 7,600 records, too many for one screener.
\item (2 Oct) Four holding categories were added at the abstract stage (context, environmental outcome only, reviews, micro evidence); automated stage-1 rules were added before manual reading; arXiv was attempted but not completed, and Scopus, Web of Science and EconLit were not searched.
\item (2--3 Oct) Records with no free full text were kept as an abstract-level map, excluded from pooled estimates and bias ratings; studies whose only venue was judged unreliable were not read.
\item (3 Oct) Four studies were moved from include to exclude after the cross-check (no own estimation, an undetermined number of economies, outcomes outside the six families, too few economies), and five regression estimates were dropped from the meta-regression.
\item (4 Oct) A blind re-screening flagged missed records; five were read, and four were included (Drama and Senou, 2025; Nguyen and Duong, 2026; \"{O}zocakl{\i}, 2026; Mialon and Nesson, 2020). A result removed by the stage-1 rule (Yin and Choi 2023) was recovered, as the term list was too narrow.
\item (Oct--Nov) Supplementary Google Scholar queries were added (G1 to G15; the eight run are in Appendix~\ref{app:search}), because the search had missed studies. Of 70 results of the seven primary-study queries, 48 were new; 31 full texts were read, 26 included and five excluded. The meta-regression, exploratory analyses and counts were rerun and earlier moderator results superseded.
\item (Nov) Because comparisons across studies could not show whether richer or poorer economies gain more, the analysis was refocused on within-study income contrasts. Backward and forward citation searching from the studies that compare income groups and a targeted OpenAlex query without a field restriction were added; only records reporting an income comparison were taken to full text. The meta-analysis of the average association and its moderator regressions became context.
\item (Nov) The World Bank macro-panel analysis was reduced to a short check (Section~\ref{sec:panel}); its full results are in the project repository.
\item (Oct) The event study was replaced by a staggered design with not-yet-treated controls after the first design failed pre-trend checks; local projections were added.
\end{enumerate}
